How to read the midterms page

Today it shows a forecast. On 3 November it turns into a scoreboard. This guide explains both: what every colour means now, and exactly what changes once votes are counted.

Before the voteWhat you are seeing now

On 3 November Americans elect all 435 members of the House, 35 of the 100 senators and 36 of the 50 governors. Nothing has been counted yet. The page therefore shows a forecast: how likely each race is to go to each party.

Seat counts include the seats not up this year. Chance of control is the share of 65,000 simulated elections in which that party ends with a majority.

The bar

The long bar is every seat in the chamber, one slice each. Slices run from the safest Democratic seat on the left to the safest Republican seat on the right, so the contested seats gather in the middle. The white line marks the majority: 218 seats in the House, 51 in the Senate, 26 of 50 governorships. A party controls the chamber if its colour reaches past the line, counted from its own end. In the Senate, a 50–50 split goes to the party of the Vice President, who casts the tie-breaking vote.

In the Senate and the governors' bar, the dimmed slices at each end are seats that are not on the ballot this year. They are already decided, so they simply count towards each side's total.

The odds bar

At the top right, the blue and red bar is each party's chance of controlling the chamber. The grey stretch in the middle is the share of simulations where neither side reaches a majority, such as a 50–50 Senate or a tie. It is not a third party.

The state map

Below the bar, the country is drawn as a grid of tiles, one per state. In the House view each tile holds one square per district (California has 52), and in the Senate and governors views each tile is a single square. Dashed, empty tiles are states with no race in that chamber this year.

One race

Click any slice, square or tile to open that race on the right: the rating, the two main candidates and each one's forecast chance of winning. That is a probability, not a share of the vote. A seat rated 97% Republican does not mean Republicans will win 97% of the ballots. The table at the foot of the page lists every race with the same numbers, and it is the best view for a screen reader or for sorting your own way. The address updates as you click, so you can share a chamber or a single race.

The sourceWhere the numbers come from

Everything on the page comes from civicAPI, a free election data service. Its forecast combines five kinds of evidence: expert ratings from the main political forecasters, betting markets, candidate fundraising, polls and outside spending. It then runs the election 65,000 times to turn those ratings into chances of winning and chances of control. We add nothing and change nothing: this page is a way of looking at their numbers.

The forecast is refreshed by civicAPI, not by us, and the page shows the date of its last update in the top bar. A forecast is a statement of likelihood. An 8% chance still happens about one time in twelve.

From 3 NovemberWhat changes when votes are counted

From the morning of election day (5 am Eastern) the page switches itself into results mode. You do not need to do anything. The top bar changes from "days to election day" to live, and the page asks for new results about once a minute while the tab is visible, and again the moment you come back to it. A tab you left open from the day before switches by itself. Polls close across the United States through the evening, so the page fills in gradually.

Behind the scenes, a small shared cache sits between the page and civicAPI, so that thousands of viewers cost civicAPI a handful of requests instead of thousands. That adds up to 30 seconds, so a result can reach your screen up to about a minute and a half after civicAPI publishes it.

1. Before anything reports

Every slice goes faded. That is deliberate: it means "no result yet", and it makes a seat that does report stand out the moment it does. Do not be alarmed if the whole page looks dim in the morning.

2. While votes are coming in

A seat with votes in but no winner declared pulses gently. Click it and the race panel shows the live count: each candidate's votes and share, and the percentage of the vote reporting. The slice keeps its place in the bar.

3. When civicAPI calls a race

The slice turns solid in the winning party's full colour: bright blue for a Democrat, bright red for a Republican, gold for an independent or third party. The race panel marks the winner with a tick. The line above the bar, Called so far, adds up the called races plus the seats that were never up, and tells you when a party has reached its majority.

4. When the result goes against the forecast

If a race is called for the party the forecast did not favour, the slice or square gets a white cap (a white dot on the map). Tossups are never marked, because the forecast had no favourite. The bar never reorders, so the forecast's order stays in view and you can see at a glance where reality departs from it.

What does not move

The chance-of-control bar stays the pre-election forecast all night. It is not re-run as votes arrive, so use Called so far for the live picture. The ratings and the order of the bar also stay fixed. Only the colour of each slice changes.

If the feed goes quiet

If civicAPI cannot be reached, the page keeps the last results it received rather than going blank, and the top bar says it is showing a cached snapshot instead of live data. Reloading the page tries again.

A few cautionsReading it carefully

  • Early counts mislead. The votes that arrive first are not a fair sample of the whole. Depending on the state, early returns can lean towards either party, so a seat showing 70% reporting is not the same as 70% decided.
  • A call is a call, not a certificate. A race is marked solid when civicAPI declares a winner. Very close races can take days, and some go to recounts, in which case the slice stays pulsing until a winner is declared.
  • Seats are not equal. Every slice looks the same size, but a Senate seat or a governorship matters far more than one House district. The bar is about counting seats, not weighing them.
  • Colours do not carry the whole story. Every seat can also be hovered for its rating and matchup, and everything is in the table, so nothing depends on telling two shades apart.
